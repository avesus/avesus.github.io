We Got It All Wrong: The Right To Create

July 31, 2026
Article published August 2, 2026

We Got It All Wrong · Season 1, Episode 1

Human time is finite. Our tools and education should expand the number of people who can create—not reserve mastery for a selected professional class while everyone else consumes the result.

The forest returns that question to first principles: what matters, what can grow, and what one human life can leave for the next generation.

The Forest Restores The Scale Of The Question

This forest is the inspiration for a new series because it strips away the shallow scale on which modern life asks us to think. A forest does not confuse a temporary interface with reality. It shows growth, succession, dependence, persistence, and time.

Walk among trees and ferns long enough and the hierarchy becomes obvious. A notification is small. A product cycle is small. A professional gate is small. A human life, a family, a body of work, and the world inherited by the next generation are large.

Nature reconnects us with what is primary and fundamental. That perspective makes the design of our technological and educational world look backward: we spend enormous effort making people easier consumers while leaving their capacity to create underdeveloped.

You Get One Lifetime To Make The Impact

Technology is often discussed as though time were unlimited. It is not. From birth to death, each person receives one finite interval in which to learn, love, build a family, create work, and leave a legacy that improves the starting conditions of another generation.

A humane technical culture would optimize for that fact. It would reduce the years required to reach the powerful idea. It would expose the structure behind the tool. It would help people move from intention to action and let them verify that the action actually happened.

Instead, many products place a smooth surface over an uncertain machine. You press a button and cannot tell whether the requested action occurred, what state changed, or what decision the system made for you. Convenience becomes helplessness when the interface removes both control and understanding.

A Phone Can Become A Camera Again

The Blackmagic Camera app offers a small but revealing reversal. A company that builds professional cinema equipment gave ordinary phone owners direct access to a serious camera interface. The phone becomes an instrument again: something a person can learn, control, and use deliberately.

That feels unusual because the dominant consumer trend has moved the other way. Capable machines are simplified until their most meaningful choices disappear. The underlying technology may be extraordinary, even free and openly available, while the person holding it is treated as someone who should select a preset and accept the result.

The barrier between consumer and professional is therefore not always a physical limit. Often it is a choice about which controls, models, and explanations a person is allowed to see. Restore those, and the same person can begin creating at a level the simplified product implied was unavailable.

Education Hides The Composition Layer

The same pattern appears in education. A learner can spend years practicing the pieces of a field without being shown the high-level composition that makes those pieces cohere.

Music makes the omission easy to hear. A student learns notes, scales, technique, and repertoire. Yet the reusable structure of a style—the relationships that make one composer or performer recognizable—can remain unnamed. People who discover an individual formula may protect it as a private advantage instead of teaching others how such formulas are found, tested, and transformed.

This is larger than music. Every field has a composition layer: the compact principles that select, connect, and direct its lower-level skills. When that layer remains hidden, learners are told to invest fifteen years and hope that mastery eventually appears. When it is taught directly, they can begin making original decisions much earlier.

Creation Cannot Remain A Professional Privilege

A culture organized around consumption reserves masterpieces for a carefully selected group and assigns everyone else the role of audience. Professionals create the camera system, song, machine, lesson, or platform. Consumers receive the finished surface, pay for access, and are encouraged to mistake constraint for inability.

That arrangement is economically useful to institutions that sell finished experiences. It is destructive to human possibility. It wastes the imagination of millions of people and turns knowledge that could compound across generations into a guarded advantage.

The answer is not to reject professional craft. It is to teach the craft’s real structure, make powerful tools legible, and let many more people cross the boundary from appreciation into authorship. More creators do not diminish mastery. They enlarge the world in which mastery can develop.

This Series Is About The Fix

We got the priorities wrong. We optimized products for passive ease, education for prolonged permission, and culture for the sale of completed experiences. We can optimize instead for agency, comprehension, creation, family, legacy, and the finite span of a human life.

The forest is the starting point because it makes superficial constraints look temporary. The deeper system is alive, connected, and capable of growth. Our tools and schools can be designed the same way: reveal the important structure, return meaningful control, and help each person make something the next generation can inherit.

That is the purpose of We Got It All Wrong: identify the backward assumption, recover what is fundamental, and build the replacement in public.
