Movie Inspirations for Programmable Matter
April 28, 2022

Long before programmable matter had an engineering roadmap, films made its consequences visible. Repairing objects, copying physical forms, changing shape, coordinating swarms, flying, and thinking through matter became a visual requirements library.

Brian Greenforest connects that imaginative lineage to a computing architecture where many local machines cooperate to create large physical behavior.

Stories Turn Possibility Into Requirements

She Fell From the Clouds, Batteries Not Included, and Stargate SG-1 foreground repair and replication. Flight of the Navigator, Terminator 2, and Transcendence show shape change and material transformation.

Prototype, Short Circuit, GoBots, Battlestar Galactica, The Matrix Revolutions, and Space Sweepers expand the frame toward robots, swarms, integrated machine worlds, and distributed intelligence.

Translate the Image Into Local Rules

Each cinematic capability becomes an engineering question: what can one cell sense, compute, move, repair, or communicate, and how do many cells coordinate without a central bottleneck?

Artists, roboticists, materials researchers, and computer architects can use these references as shared design language. Choose one scene, identify the local behaviors it requires, and build the mechanism that brings it closer.

The sources of inspiration were found in the movies: She Fell From Clouds (1978) on fixing things and replicating physical objects, Prototype (1983) and Short Circuit (1986) on general robotics (and a ton of Soviet books on robotics, programming, and electronics!), Flight of the Navigator (1986) on flying and transforming shapes, Octopuses From the Second Floor (1986) on sentient beings, malleable to have arbitrary functions, Batteries Not Included (1987) on fixing things again, GoBots (1987), Terminator 2: Judgment Day (1991), and Battlestar Galactica (2003 miniseries) as a continuation on malleable thinking matter, Stargate SG-1 5.19 (2002) on self-replicating robots, The Matrix Revolutions (2003) on the vision of an integrated robotic universe, Transcendence (2014) and Space Sweepers (2021) on the best visuals of nanorobots in action.
