AI Should Interview Before It Acts

Developed July 26, 2020 and July 31, 2020. Updated July 28, 2026.

Interview-first agents

The hardest part of helping someone comes before the answer: discovering which problem that person actually needs to solve.

An agent should ask, listen, build several probable futures, and compare how the person fares in each one. Only then has it earned the right to recommend a product, plan, job, course, or action.

The valuable machine does more than produce new material. It finds the need beneath the request and gives the person a clearer choice.

The Goal Must Come From The Person

Language models can draft requirements from plain English. Planning systems can search for actions under competing objectives. Execution systems can carry out a selected sequence. Every one of those capabilities begins after the decisive question: what should the system try to achieve?

In business, the hard question rarely concerns proposal mechanics. It concerns what the client wants, what they can use, what they fear losing, and which constraint nobody has spoken aloud. Marketing defines a population. Sales offers a product line. The interview reveals the life of the person across the table.

Precise action starts with a precise problem, yet most automated systems hurry past its discovery.

Interviewing Is Part Of The Machine

A good interviewer notices hesitation, narrows a question, checks an interpretation, and lets each answer change the next question. Podcast hosts, journalists, consultants, teachers, coaches, and strong salespeople practice this loop until it feels conversational. Beneath that ease lies a disciplined information-gathering process.

An agent needs the same loop:

  1. Ask what the person is trying to change.
  2. Listen for goals, constraints, history, skills, obligations, and uncertainty.
  3. State its understanding in concrete terms and invite correction.
  4. Construct more than one plausible next situation.
  5. Explain the benefits, costs, risks, and unknowns in each situation.
  6. Ask again before choosing or acting.

The conversation makes the recommendation fit this person instead of an average assembled from a dataset.

Compare The Person, Not The Things

Utility becomes confused when a system ranks two things as though one object carried universal good and another universal harm. A choice has value only through the situation it creates for a particular person.

The agent must therefore compare that person in state A with the same person in state B. What can they do in each state? What becomes easier? Which obligation grows? What can they reverse? Which outcome depends on an assumption?

This comparison is betterness: an open account of possible experiences, including the dimensions that refuse to collapse honestly into one score.

Wanting and being able differ. A promotion may attract someone while consuming the time they need to care for another person. A powerful tool may fail to fit the existing work. A cheap product may demand expensive daily attention. The state surrounding the choice belongs inside the choice.

Run Branches Before Spending A Life

People already imagine branches. Psychologists use mentalization to describe how we interpret our own and other people’s mental states. Engineers can bring that attention to perspective into alternative-future simulation. Physics supplies the wonderfully excessive image of many worlds. An agent needs the practical lesson: more than one future deserves consideration.

The conversation can generate several probable continuations. In one, the person buys the product. In another, they keep the present arrangement. In a third, they solve the underlying problem without buying anything. Each branch exposes its assumptions, consequences, and uncertainty for inspection.

The branches make hidden consequences discussable before a decision closes them off.

Sales Is Only One Instance

The original product idea began with sales because sales makes the missing interview painfully visible. A salesperson can reach beyond one organization’s catalog, develop real mastery across several niches, and help a client see the problem before matching it to any supplier.

The same platform can support several roles:

  • Media makers and broadcasters can interview people until a problem becomes understandable to an audience, then carry useful follow-up questions into the next conversation.
  • Consultants and searchers can discover the requirement before searching for a solution.
  • Deliverers can make fulfillment part of the promised future from the beginning.
  • Personal and organizational coaches can connect goals with priorities, effort, resources, present work, and open commitments.
  • Hiring and placement systems can reason from a person's actual history and skills instead of matching isolated keywords.
  • Teachers, trainers, examiners, and certifiers can treat education as resource management across time, attention, practice, evidence, and opportunity.

These roles need the same discipline: understand before prescribing.

The Interview Must Serve The Person

Entrepreneurship exposes how easily a system can confuse care for a person with care for a conversion. An interview-first agent betrays its purpose when it uses intimate understanding only to sharpen persuasion.

The person must control the goal and retain the power to inspect and correct the agent’s model. The agent should collect private answers only when the task needs them and retain them only with permission. It should name conflicts of interest and respect refusal or silence.

A system that serves the person must retain the option of recommending no purchase at all.

The Agent Earns The Right To Act

The machine should ask, listen, and imagine probable good and bad experiences with us. It should reveal problems that resist a first description, compare futures openly, and return control to the person who must live in the chosen branch.

Fluent conversation starts the work. Earned, person-specific action completes it.
