Encanto Removed the Villain. Now Remove Power.

August 2026

Cultural critique

A beautiful film showed how love can become control - and then rebuilt the house that made it happen.

"A gift that cannot be refused is not a gift."

An essay on family, work, refusal, and a world without command

For generations, Disney taught children to wait for the villain. The villain had a poisoned gift, a cruel laugh, an army at the gate, or a forbidden room. Defeat that one bad person, and the world became good again. The rightful ruler returned. The family went home. Order was restored.

The Evil Queen fell. Maleficent was defeated. Ursula was destroyed. Scar was thrown from Pride Rock. Jafar was trapped in a lamp. Mother Gothel turned to dust. The lesson was comforting: evil belongs to an evil person. Remove the person, and there is nothing wrong with the throne, the kingdom, the family, or the command.

Then came Encanto. No villain entered the house. Bruno was blamed, but he was not evil. Mirabel was not the threat. Alma caused terrible harm, but she was not a monster. The armed men who killed Pedro belonged to Alma's memory; no invader drove the present story. The danger came from ordinary love twisted by fear, duty, gratitude, and obedience.

Encanto is Disney's first truly wonderful story of a new kind: a story in which the expected villain never arrives and the missing villain is the point. The family is not saved by finding an enemy. It is saved by discovering that people can hurt one another while believing they are doing what is best. For one astonishing moment, Disney stops asking, "Who is evil?" It asks, "What if the way we live is hurting us?"

That question is greater than any villain. But the film does not follow it to the end. It breaks the house, opens the truth, and brings the family to the edge of a new world. Then it turns back. It rebuilds the same house. It restores the gifts. It repairs love but leaves the old order standing. Encanto removes the villain. Now the story must remove power.

What Power Really Is

Power is often described as strength, courage, talent, leadership, or the ability to act. Those things are not power. The strength to carry a stone is not power. The courage to speak is not power. The ability to sing, build, teach, heal, travel, refuse, or leave is not power. These are human abilities. They do not require anyone to stand above anyone else.

Power begins with a claim: I have the right to tell you what to do. It becomes real when refusal has a cost: if you do not obey, I can take something from you.

The cost may be money, shelter, food, safety, status, freedom, affection, approval, belonging, or peace. It may be a slap, a threat, a humiliation, a lost job, a locked door, a cold silence, or a family that suddenly stops calling you family. The final threat behind command is violence, but power usually works before violence becomes visible. People learn to predict the cost and obey early.

Power is not the ability to act. Power is the claimed right to direct another person's actions and make that person pay for refusing.

This claim hides inside respectable roles. The parent says, "I provide for you, so you obey." The teacher says, "I know more than you, so you obey." The mentor says, "I helped you, so follow my path." The employer says, "I pay you, so your time belongs to me." The husband says, "I protect this family, so I decide." The state says, "I keep order, so do not resist."

The words change. The claim does not: I give something, therefore I own something in you. Care stops being care when it sends a bill called obedience.

People can guide, help, teach, protect, and make plans together without claiming ownership of one another. A request can be urgent. A reason can be strong. A danger can be real. None of that creates a permanent right to punish another person's no.

A Gift That Cannot Be Refused

Every Madrigal child receives a magical gift at the age of five, except Mirabel. The ceremony looks joyful. A door appears. The family celebrates. The child receives a room and a place in the story. But the gift quickly becomes a duty, and the duty becomes an identity. A person becomes a job.

Luisa is strong, so everyone gives her burdens. Her body becomes public equipment. People do not ask what she wants to carry. They ask what else she can carry. Her song tells us that she is close to breaking, yet the family and the town have built daily life around her refusal being unthinkable. Luisa's strength belongs to everyone except Luisa.

Pepa's feelings change the weather, so her feelings become a public problem. She must calm down for everyone else's comfort. She is not simply allowed to feel. The family treats her inner life as dangerous weather that must be managed.

Dolores can hear everything, so her private sense becomes the family's information service. Isabela creates flowers, so she must be beautiful, graceful, agreeable, and perfect. Her future marriage is arranged around the family's image. Perfection makes her easy to arrange.

Camilo can change his appearance, so he is useful whenever someone needs another face or another performance. Bruno sees possible futures, so he is blamed when the future frightens people. He learns that when truth scares a family, the truth-teller becomes the problem. He hides inside the walls, close enough to care for everyone but unable to live among them.

Mirabel receives no magical gift. She remains in the nursery. She is omitted from the family photograph and treated as a risk to the miracle. Yet her exclusion lets her see what everyone else has learned not to see: Luisa is exhausted, Isabela is trapped, Bruno is hiding, Pepa fears her own feelings, and the house is cracking.

A gift that cannot be refused is not a gift. It is an assignment.

A talent that must always be used for others is a duty. A duty enforced by shame, fear, or exclusion is a command. The Madrigals call it service. Every society has a beautiful word for obedience.

Alma's Wound Does Not Make Her a Ruler

Alma's story matters. She was driven from her home. She watched Pedro die while protecting her and their children. She crossed a river in terror. The miracle gave displaced people safety, a home, and a community. Alma became determined never to lose any of it again. Her fear is not foolish. Her pain is not imaginary. Her love is real.

Alma's suffering explains her control. It does not justify her control.

A person who survived hunger does not gain ownership of everyone else's food. A person who survived danger does not gain ownership of everyone else's choices. A person who built a home does not gain ownership of the people inside it. Pain can explain why someone becomes controlling. It cannot make control rightful.

Alma protects the miracle by turning every family member into part of a plan: be strong, be perfect, be useful, do not upset the family, do not shame the miracle, do not waste your gift. These commands do not arrive as hatred. They arrive as love, gratitude, duty, and sacrifice. That is why they are hard to refuse.

The family obeys because they love Alma and know what she endured. They do not want to hurt her. They do not want to seem selfish. They do not want to lose their place in the family. Love becomes the rope that holds everyone still.

Encanto reveals a truth that family stories often avoid: control does not need a cruel face. It can cook dinner. It can tell stories about sacrifice. It can kiss your forehead and still refuse to let you belong to yourself.

Mirabel Should Not Have Had to Save Them

Mirabel has no magical room. She is pushed out of important moments. Alma treats her questions as threats. When the house cracks, Mirabel is blamed for noticing. Still, Mirabel becomes responsible for saving the family that denied her a full place in it.

She comforts Luisa, frees Isabela from compulsory perfection, searches for Bruno, risks herself to understand the vision, confronts Alma, follows her to the river, listens to the story of Pedro, forgives, returns, and helps rebuild. Mirabel is denied a door, then made responsible for saving the house that judged her unworthy.

This pattern is painfully familiar. The person harmed by a family, school, workplace, or country is asked to explain the harm gently. She must understand everyone's fear, remain patient, find the right words, forgive, and guide the people who wounded her toward a better version of themselves. If she refuses, she is angry. If she leaves, she is selfish. If she stops helping, she is accused of dividing the family.

No child should have to heal an entire family to prove that she belongs in it.

A free Mirabel could tell the truth and decline the repair job. She could leave without becoming a traitor. Bruno could stay away without becoming a failed brother. Belonging would remain after refusal. Love would not be a prize awarded for usefulness, forgiveness, silence, or rescue.

They Rebuild the House

Casita falls after Mirabel finally confronts Alma. The magic disappears. For a moment, nobody is exceptional. The family cannot solve the crisis through inherited gifts. Then the townspeople arrive and rebuild beside them with ordinary hands. This is one of the film's most generous ideas: the community returns the care it received.

Alma apologizes. Mirabel and Bruno are welcomed. Isabela rejects compulsory perfection. The final family photograph includes everyone. These changes matter. The people become more honest and more loving.

Then Mirabel places a new doorknob on the front door. Casita returns. The gifts return. The same grand house remains at the center of village life. The film never shows Alma surrendering the right to decide what the family must be. It creates no right to refuse service and no protection for the next child who does not fit.

The people change. The structure does not change enough.

An apology can heal a relationship. It cannot, by itself, redesign a hierarchy. A kinder leader is still a leader if everyone else's safety depends on her kindness. A gentle command is still a command. Love does not turn obedience into freedom.

This is the old ending in warmer clothes: the boss listens, the father apologizes, the teacher softens, the ruler shows mercy, and everyone returns to place. They rebuild the house. They do not rebuild the rules.

The Family Prepares the Worker

The first place most people learn power is not a palace or a prison. It is the home. A child is born needing care. Adults must feed, shelter, guide, and protect that child. From this necessary care, an unnecessary claim is often created: because the adult is responsible for the child, the adult owns the child's choices.

Care becomes command. Protection becomes control. Gratitude becomes debt. The child learns that peace depends on pleasing the person in charge. Adults may demand explanations; children hear, "Because I said so." Children are people before they are projects.

School often continues the lesson. Sit where you are told. Speak when permitted. Ask to use the bathroom. Compete for approval. Treat time as someone else's property. The good student is quiet, punctual, productive, and easy to manage. Knowledge becomes mixed with obedience until they look like the same thing.

Then work gives the lesson a salary. Many companies call themselves families. The phrase sounds warm, but it carries an old demand: sacrifice for us, stay late, accept less, smile, be loyal, do not embarrass the group, and do not question the person who signs your pay.

Pay buys the work we agree to do. It does not buy the person doing it.

Money does not buy the right to humiliate, invade, silence, threaten, or command beyond an honest agreement. Yet millions of people must tolerate these things because refusal may cost housing, healthcare, food, or safety. That is not free cooperation. It is obedience with a paycheck.

For centuries, Western culture dressed command as masculine courage. Men were praised for taking charge, conquering uncertainty, mastering land and sea, winning women, leading rooms, and forcing order onto resistance. Women and children were praised for patience, loyalty, support, and compliance. Patriarchy taught men to command and taught women to make command feel like care. A woman can sit in the old chair, but equal access to the chair is not freedom.

No secret group had to design this lesson. Old rules survive whenever a story presents them as the natural shape of love. The hidden lesson lives in the choices an ending refuses to imagine. Encanto allows the family to speak about pressure, but not to question why any person must make a gift continuously available. That is how a beautiful film prepares a child for a beautiful workplace that asks for the same surrender.

Human Rights Began the Work

Human-rights movements changed the world. They challenged slavery, conquest, segregation, child labor, marital ownership, religious persecution, torture, and the legal exclusion of women. They won rights to vote, learn, organize, speak, leave marriages, own property, and live without forms of violence once defended as natural. These victories are real. They were bought with courage and suffering.

But the work remains unfinished. Human rights often told the person in charge what he must not do. They did not always remove the belief that someone must be in charge. The father could no longer beat his wife, but he remained head of the household. The employer could no longer openly discriminate, but still controlled whether workers could pay rent. The teacher could no longer strike a child, but still ruled the room.

Human rights limited the master. The next freedom removes the master.

The next step rejects the belief that care creates ownership, knowledge creates rank, money creates a right to command, protection requires obedience, or coordination requires a permanent superior. It gives each person a life that remains livable after saying no.

We do not need better masters. We need relationships without masters.

What the New World Requires

Freedom begins when refusal is survivable.

A child who says no must not lose love. A partner who leaves must not lose safety. A worker who refuses abuse must not lose healthcare and housing. A student who questions a teacher must not lose dignity. A family member who rejects a role must not be cast out. Care must remain after disagreement.

Parenthood must become care without ownership. Adults should protect children from immediate harm, but protection is a duty, not a throne. Pulling a child away from traffic does not create a lifelong right to direct that child's body, feelings, friendships, work, love, or future. The adult succeeds when the child can think, choose, and leave safely.

Teaching must offer knowledge without rule. A teacher may know more about a subject without becoming more human. Students should help shape the rules that govern their days. Education should prepare people to question, choose, cooperate, and create - not to wait for instructions.

Mentorship must give tools without collecting followers. Help must not create a debt of loyalty. A mentor succeeds when the learner can disagree, take another path, and walk away freely.

Work must become a clear agreement among equals. Pay must purchase a defined contribution, not silence, personality, gratitude, or general obedience. Food, housing, medical care, and legal safety must not hang from one supervisor's approval. Work that nobody freely accepts must be shared differently, redesigned, delayed, automated, paid better, or abandoned.

People can still make plans together. They can choose coordinators, divide tasks, set rules for shared spaces, and remove someone who misuses a role. But the role must be narrow, temporary, visible, and easy to revoke. A coordinator may carry a task. A coordinator may not own the people doing it.

When harm is immediate, people may block it. We pull someone away from a fire. We stop an attack. We hold a collapsing wall. But an emergency creates a duty to help, not a title to rule. Saving a life does not make that life yours.

The test of freedom is not how warmly a society accepts yes. It is what happens after no. A free society does not punish refusal with hunger, homelessness, humiliation, exile, lost care, or lost love. It changes the plan.

The Ending Encanto Needed

Let Casita remain fallen for a while. Let the empty ground become a place where the family must decide, without a miracle and without one ruler, what to build.

Let Alma place the candle on the ground and step away from it. Let her say, "I was afraid, and I made my fear into your duty. I had no right." Let Mirabel refuse to become the new matriarch. She can love her family without inheriting the chair that excluded her.

Let the village build many homes instead of one grand house at the center. Add common kitchens, gardens, workshops, shelters, and meeting places that belong to everyone. Let safety be shared so nobody must obey one family to receive it.

Let every gift belong to the person who carries it. Luisa may lift a bridge, or she may say no. If she says no, the town delays the journey, uses ropes and wheels, brings many hands, and builds a bridge that does not require one exceptional body. Nobody calls her selfish.

Pepa may be sad, and people bring umbrellas. Dolores may close a door and stop listening. Isabela may grow fruit, thorns, weeds, flowers, or nothing at all. Bruno may speak a frightening truth without becoming responsible for it. Antonio may enjoy animals without turning wonder into work.

The new freedom needs a real test. Something important must fail after a person refuses. The story must not punish the refuser or arrange a disaster to prove that obedience was necessary. Everyone else must adapt. That is how the audience learns that no can change the plan without ending love.

Let every person have a door. Let no one else keep the key. At the end, Alma comes to Mirabel's door and knocks. She does not enter because she is the grandmother, because she suffered, or because she built the first home. She waits.

Mirabel may say yes. Mirabel may say no. Mirabel may say, "Not yet." And the family remains a family.

That would be the miracle.

Remove the Chair

Encanto gave us something rare. It taught us to stop searching for a wicked person and look at the way ordinary love can become a command. It showed a house cracking under the weight of duty. It let the rejected child speak and made the grandmother listen. That is why the film deserves wonder.

But wonder is not enough. The next story must show that apology is the beginning of change, not the end. It must imagine love without rank, care without ownership, work without submission, teaching without command, and safety without a ruler.

No one owns your gift. No one owns your labor. No one owns your body. No one owns your future. No one owns your love. No one owns your no.

Refusal is not an attack. It is not betrayal. It is not selfishness. It is the border that proves a human being belongs to herself.

Claim what belongs to you: the right to reject power, including the idea that someone must always hold it. Do not ask for a better throne. Do not wait for a kinder person to sit in it. Do not fight for your turn in the chair.

Remove the chair.

We do not need another villain to defeat. We need a world in which nobody may become another person's owner, however loving, frightened, wise, generous, wounded, or well-intentioned that owner may be.

We do not need a nicer house. We need doors that open from the inside.
